## Sources and attribution

This guide combines established theater games with exercises adapted for a
shared set of acting goals.

- **Established formats:** Change/New Choice; Yes, And; What Are You Doing?;
  story-building; space-object work; and give-and-take focus.
- **Adapted formats:** Fortunately/Unfortunately, the physical extension of
  Yes, And, and The Unexpected Object.
- **Created for this practice plan:** Make Me Respond and Interrupted Plan.
  These apply established improv principles to the goal of responding outwardly
  when another character disrupts prepared work.

The primary lineage is Viola Spolin's theater-games approach, which uses a
specific game problem to direct attention away from self-conscious performance
and toward interaction. The official Spolin resources describe this as
learning through direct experience with fellow players rather than through
approval, disapproval, or rote instruction:

- [Viola Spolin - Playing Creates Community](https://spolin.com/?p=603)
- [Viola Spolin - Games](https://spolin.com/?page_id=10)
- [Spolin Games Online - Approval/Disapproval Syndrome](https://spolingamesonline.org/the-approval-disapproval-syndrom/)
- [Northwestern University Press - *Theater Games for the Classroom*](https://nupress.northwestern.edu/9780810163126/theater-games-for-the-classroom/)

Each game below includes one sentence explaining the skill its rule develops.
Use that sentence to choose a game, then stop thinking about it while playing.

## Purpose

This is a short, playful practice plan for Sage and Paige to use together. The
games give either actor a chance to build these skills:

- release prepared work once a scene begins;
- respond to the other actor instead of monitoring the performance;
- make clear physical and comic choices;
- try choices before deciding whether they will work;
- recover easily when a choice is surprising or imperfect; and
- expand the range between controlled characters and uninhibited characters.

Thoughtful preparation, strong singing, and controlled acting are strengths.
The goal is not to replace either person's style or change anyone's
personality. It is to make spontaneity, physical freedom, and comic risk
additional tools each actor can use when a character or director calls for
them.

Sage and Paige bring different strengths to the games, and those strengths may
change from game to game. Both should practice initiating, receiving, leaving
space, responding visibly, and allowing the other actor to change the scene.
Neither person is the permanent coach or helper; each game is shared practice.

## Practice principles

1. **Play rather than polish.** The session is for discovering choices, not
   preparing a finished performance.
2. **Let the partner cause the response.** Look, listen, and allow new
   information to change the body, voice, objective, or tactic.
3. **Commit before evaluating.** Make the choice fully and continue the scene.
4. **Keep the scene moving.** Unexpected moments become part of the scene.
5. **Use physical action for a reason.** Movement should pursue an objective,
   respond to the partner, or affect the environment.
6. **Share the work equally.** Alternate who begins, interrupts, introduces
   complications, and receives the challenge.
7. **End while it is still fun.** Ten to fifteen minutes is plenty.

## A simple session

### 1. Arrive - 2 minutes

- Shake out arms and legs.
- Walk through the room at different speeds.
- Take turns leading changes in direction, level, rhythm, and use of space.
- Follow each change immediately without discussing it.

### 2. Play - 8 to 10 minutes

Choose one or two games from this guide. Switch roles at least once.

### 3. Notice - 1 minute

Each partner completes one sentence:

> When you did **___**, it made me **___**.

Examples:

- "When you stepped between me and the door, it made me try to get around
  you."
- "When you suddenly protected the backpack, it made me want to know what was
  inside."
- "When you stopped and looked directly at me, it made me change what I was
  going to say."

The feedback describes the effect on the partner. It is not a score and does
not decide which performance was best.

### 4. Finish

Name one game to repeat another day, then stop. Detailed acting notes are for a
teacher or director.

## Game 1: Make Me Respond

**Goal:** Allow another person to cause an immediate, visible change.

One partner supplies something the other cannot prepare in advance. The
receiving actor must notice the new information and let it produce a visible
response before planning an ideal one. Switching roles lets both actors
practice creating clear offers and responding to them.

**Source:** A custom exercise based on shared practice goals and Spolin's focus
on actors giving full attention to one another. Related established Spolin
games include **Camera** and **Contact** on the
[official Spolin games page](https://spolin.com/?page_id=10).

1. Choose a simple relationship and problem.
2. Give each character something they want from the other person.
3. Begin the scene.
4. One actor introduces an unexpected action or piece of information.
5. The other actor lets it change something immediately: movement, distance,
   focus, tactic, use of an object, or tone.
6. Continue for 20 to 30 seconds.
7. Switch who introduces the unexpected change.

Possible situations:

- One friend needs the other to return something important.
- Two performers both want the only available costume piece.
- One person is trying to hide a surprise while the other needs help.
- Two students have been given conflicting instructions.

## Game 2: Change!

**Goal:** Generate and commit to new choices without searching for the perfect
one.

The call to change removes an actor's first answer and requires the scene to
continue. Each partner gets experience releasing a choice without treating it
as a failure, then accepting the other person's new reality.

**Source:** An established improv game usually called **New Choice** or
**Change**. See
[Hoopla Impro - New Choice / Change](https://www.hooplaimpro.com/new-choice.html),
which describes its purposes as spontaneity, adapting to change, and
incorporating mistakes.

1. Begin a simple scene.
2. At unpredictable moments, the partner says, "Change!"
3. The actor immediately replaces the most recent statement or action with a
   different one.
4. The new choice becomes true, and both actors continue from it.
5. Alternate who performs and who calls the changes.

Example:

> "I brought everything we need for the picnic."
>
> "Change!"
>
> "I accidentally gave our picnic to a marching band."
>
> "Change!"
>
> "Our picnic basket is making a strange noise."

Call "Change!" based on timing and surprise rather than judging the quality of
the previous choice.

## Game 3: Fortunately / Unfortunately

**Goal:** Accept a partner's idea and adapt without returning to the original
plan.

Each new fact makes the previous plan less usable, so both actors practice
accepting a changed situation and building forward instead of trying to restore
the story they expected.

**Source:** A common alternating-story variation of the established
**Building a Story** format. See **Building a Story** on the
[official Spolin games page](https://spolin.com/?page_id=10).

Build a story one sentence at a time:

1. One actor begins with "Fortunately..."
2. The other continues with "Unfortunately..."
3. Continue alternating.
4. Allow each new fact to redirect the story.
5. On a later round, add physical reactions while telling it.
6. Switch who starts the next story.

Example:

> "Fortunately, we arrived at school early."
>
> "Unfortunately, the school is now underwater."
>
> "Fortunately, the orchestra room has scuba equipment."

## Game 4: Yes, And - Physical Edition

**Goal:** Receive new information with both the imagination and the body.

Requiring each accepted fact to affect the body makes the partner's offer
visible instead of allowing the scene to remain only a clever verbal exchange.
Both actors practice making offers that matter and allowing those offers to
change them.

**Source:** **Yes, And** is a foundational improv principle taught by
[The Second City](https://www.secondcity.com/classes/virtual/improv/improv-1-online-vir)
to develop agreement, listening, presence, spontaneity, risk-taking, and the
making and acceptance of offers. Requiring each new fact to have a physical
effect is an adaptation for these shared practice goals.

1. Establish a place and relationship.
2. One actor introduces a new fact.
3. The partner accepts it, adds to it, and lets it change something physical.
4. Continue building the same shared reality.
5. Switch who introduces the first fact.

Example:

> "The floor is slowly turning into pudding."

The response might affect balance, movement, urgency, distance, the use of
furniture, or what the character needs from the partner.

## Game 5: What Are You Doing?

**Goal:** Begin a physical choice immediately.

Each actor begins the named activity before designing exactly how it should
look, practicing physical commitment ahead of analysis while also giving a
partner a clear, playable suggestion.

**Source:** An established youth drama game. See
[Drama Notebook - What Are You Doing?](https://www.dramanotebook.com/drama-games/what-are-you-doing/).

1. One actor starts performing a clear physical activity.
2. The partner asks, "What are you doing?"
3. The first actor names a different safe activity.
4. The partner immediately begins performing the named activity.
5. The first actor asks, "What are you doing?"
6. Continue alternating.

Use the whole body and enough detail that the activity becomes recognizable.

## Game 6: The Unexpected Object

**Goal:** Commit quickly and let a shared imaginary object affect the scene.

Specific weight, size, texture, and use give the receiving actor's body a
concrete task. The partner's added complication keeps both actors focused on
the shared object rather than on whether either person looks expressive.

**Source:** An adaptation of established Spolin space-object work. See
[Spolin Games Online - Object Moves the Players](https://spolingamesonline.org/object-moves-the-players/)
and [Drama Notebook - Space Objects](https://www.dramanotebook.com/drama-games/space-objects/).

1. One actor hands the other an imaginary object without naming it.
2. The receiving actor decides what it is through weight, size, texture, and
   use.
3. The giver accepts that choice and adds one fact or complication.
4. Continue until the object has changed the relationship or problem.
5. Switch roles.

Possible objects include:

- a box containing something alive;
- a very delicate award;
- a costume piece that will not cooperate;
- a machine with one surprising function; or
- an ordinary object that has become extremely important.

## Game 7: Interrupted Plan

**Goal:** Let an interruption land, change the next thought, and send the
response back to the partner.

The game rehearses a simple sequence: an interruption lands, something
changes, and the receiving actor directs the next thought back to the partner
without restarting or privately preparing the response. Both actors practice
the sequence by switching roles.

**Source:** A custom exercise combining a clear objective, an unpredictable
partner offer, and the Spolin principle that actors solve a specific problem
through direct interaction. The underlying approach is described in
[Viola Spolin - Playing Creates Community](https://spolin.com/?p=603).

1. One actor begins with a clear plan and objective.
2. The partner listens and chooses an unexpected interruption.
3. The first actor lets the interruption affect them before continuing.
4. The next line must be directed back to the partner rather than delivered
   privately.
5. Continue the scene without restarting.
6. Switch roles so both actors practice disrupting and receiving.

The interruption can change:

- what the character thinks just happened;
- how close the characters are;
- who has control;
- what object matters;
- how urgent the problem feels; or
- what tactic the character tries next.

## Game 8: Give and Take Focus

**Goal:** Make stage attention a shared, intentional resource.

Passing focus asks each actor to make an impulse visible and then become
available for the partner's next impulse. Both practice outward connection,
clear initiation, and leaving space without requiring constant movement.

**Source:** **Give-and-take** is an established Spolin principle. Her official
games page describes it as foundational to the work. A classroom version is
available at
[Drama Notebook - Give and Take](https://www.dramanotebook.com/drama-games/give-and-take/).

1. Begin a silent activity together.
2. One actor takes focus through a clear action.
3. That actor then gives focus to the partner through eye contact, movement,
   an offered object, or a change in position.
4. Continue passing focus without speaking.
5. Add dialogue only after the silent exchange feels clear.

## Partner feedback

Feedback should remain short, descriptive, and connected to the scene.

This feedback format is a custom application of Spolin's nonjudgmental
evaluation principle. Its purpose is to notice whether the game's focus became
visible, rather than to reward or criticize either actor. See
[Spolin Games Online - Approval/Disapproval Syndrome](https://spolingamesonline.org/the-approval-disapproval-syndrom/).

Useful questions:

- What did your partner do that changed your plan?
- What did your partner make you want to do?
- When did the relationship change?
- What action made the objective clearest?
- What surprised you?
- What new fact did you have to accept?

One observation from each actor is enough. The next game provides the next
chance to experiment.

## Signs the practice is helping

Progress may look like:

- each actor responds before privately planning the ideal response;
- unexpected information produces a visible change;
- physical choices remain connected to the partner and objective;
- each actor continues easily after an awkward or unsuccessful idea;
- both actors explore several possibilities without ranking them first;
- both actors leave room for the other person to initiate and affect the scene;
- both listen closely instead of planning their next contribution;
- laughter supports the shared scene rather than stopping it; and
- the games remain enjoyable for both friends.

The goal is not for either actor to become constantly bigger or sillier. The
goal is to remain available: controlled when control serves the character and
free, physical, or ridiculous when the scene asks for it.

## Using this work in auditions and rehearsal

The games should expand each actor's available responses, not predetermine a
performance.

In rehearsal:

- listen fully to direction;
- try the requested choice before analyzing it;
- let scene partners cause real changes;
- explore both rooted, controlled work and uninhibited comic play; and
- notice how contrasting character worlds can inform each other.

In a future audition:

- prepare the material thoroughly;
- know the listener, objective, obstacle, and turn;
- allow the imagined partner to disrupt the preparation;
- use contrasting pieces when broader casting range matters; and
- treat an unexpected moment as something to play rather than something to
  correct.

## Frequency

- Play for **10 to 15 minutes once or twice a week**.
- Use only one or two games in a session.
- Switch roles and leadership.
- Record rarely and only when both actors want to review a specific skill.
- Let regular rehearsals and directors provide the primary performance
  coaching.
